01 · Overview

Engineering a Wearable Character System.

The goal of this project is not simply to reproduce the appearance of the Mark 7 armor. The larger challenge is translating the highly stylized fictional design into a wearable physical system that can be fabricated, assembled, worn, maintained, and progressively integrated with mechanical and electronic functions.

The project combines digital modeling, additive manufacturing, mechanical packaging, body fitment, finishing, electronics, motion systems, and iterative testing.

02 · Experience Intent

Making the Character Feel Real.

My design goal is to create more than a static costume. I want the final system to communicate the feeling of interacting with a real powered suit through movement, lighting, mechanical response, physical presence, and eventually additional interactive functions.

That makes the project an immersive systems problem: the mechanical structure, electronics, visual design, ergonomics, sound, lighting, and user interaction all contribute to the final experience.

04 · Digital Fabrication

Turning Complex Geometry into Manufacturable Parts.

The digital armor geometry must be divided into components that can realistically be manufactured, assembled, finished, repaired, and worn.

Part orientation and segmentation affect print time, support material, strength, surface quality, assembly, and the ability to package future electronics or mechanisms inside the armor.

05 · Ergonomics & Fitment

Designing Around The Human (My) Body.

Wearability introduces constraints that do not exist in the digital model. Armor sections must maintain the intended appearance while still allowing movement, visibility, comfort, access, and sufficient clearance between adjacent components.

Fit testing helps identify interference points and determines where geometry, attachment methods, articulation, or scaling needs to change.

06 · Mechanical Architecture

Building Movement Into The Armor.

One of the most interesting challenges is determining which components should remain rigid and which require controlled movement or articulation.

Mechanical design decisions must consider available packaging space, range of motion, actuator force, attachment strength, serviceability, wiring paths, weight, and reliability.

Future and current development areas include motorized armor functions, helmet movement, controlled panel motion, and other interactive mechanisms.

07 · Electronics & Mechatronics

Adding Intelligence To The Physical System.

The mechatronics portion of the project explores how controllers, sensors, actuators, lighting, power, and user inputs can be integrated into a wearable platform.

The long-term architecture allows individual armor functions to operate as subsystems while remaining capable of being coordinated through a larger control system.

08 · Iteration

Each Version Exposes The Next Problem.

The suit has evolved through multiple revisions as issues with fitment, fabrication, assembly, mobility, mechanical packaging, and overall presentation become visible.

Instead of treating failed parts or design changes as setbacks, I use each version as a physical prototype that provides information for the next iteration.

09 · Testing

Testing The System Before Adding Complexity.

Mechanical and electronic functions are tested independently before full integration whenever possible.

This makes it easier to isolate problems involving fitment, wiring, power, actuator behavior, component interference, or mechanical load without having to troubleshoot the entire suit at once.

10 · Current Development

Expanding From Wearable Armor into an Interactive System.

The current direction is focused on continuing the fabrication and finishing process while expanding the suit's mechatronic capabilities.

Future development may include additional motorized functions, improved electronic integration, lighting, control logic, improved internal packaging, and more coordinated interaction between armor subsystems.

Reflection

What This Project Has Taught Me.

This project has reinforced how closely mechanical design, electronics, manufacturing, human factors, and visual design are connected in a wearable system.

A component can look correct in CAD and still fail once it interacts with a real person, another armor component, a wiring harness, or a moving mechanism.

Building the suit has pushed me to think about an engineered object not only as a collection of parts, but as an experience created by many systems working together. Lastly, this stuff is expensive!
